# 2.2.7

> Here is a more constructive definition of the transitive closure of a relation
> $R$. First we define the following sequence of sets of pairs:
>
> $$
> R_{0} = R
> $$
>
> and
>
> $$
> R_{i+1} = R_{i} \cup \{(s,u) |t\in S;(s,t)\in R_{i}, (t,u)\in R_{i}\}
> $$
>
> That is, we construct each $R_{i+1}$ by adding to $R_{i}$ all the pairs that
> can be obtained by "one step transitivity" from pairs already in $R_{i}$.
> Finally, define the relation $R^{+}$ as the union of all the $R_{i}$:
>
> $$
> R^{+} = \bigcup_{i} R_{i}
> $$
>
> Show that $R^{+}$ really is the transitive closure of $R$.

## Lemma

We first show a little lemma

> If for a certain $i\in N$ we have $(a,b)\in R_{i}$ then for all $j\ge i$
> we have $(a,b)\in R_{j}$.

### Proof

We call a number $n$ cute if $(a,b)\in R_{i+n}$. Since $(a,b)\in R_{i}$ zero is
cute.

Assume $k$ to be cute, we will show that $k+1$ is cute as well. Since $k$ is
cute we have that $(a,b)\in R_{i+k}$. Notice that

$$
R_{i+1}=R_{i} \cup \{(s,u)|t\in S;(s,t)\in R_{i}, (t,u)\in R_{i}\}
$$

means that $R{i+k}\subset R_{i+k+1}$ hence $(a,b)\in R_{i+k+1}$ so $k+1$ is
cute.

By the principle of mathematical induction all natural numbers are cute. I.e.
$(a,b)\in R_{j}$ for all $j\ge i$.

## Proof

A relation $E$ is transitive if for pairs $(s,t), (t,u)\in E$ we have
$(s,u)\in E$.

Let $(s,t), (t,u)\in R^{+}$. Then there are $i,j\in N$ such that
$(s,t)\in R_{i}$ and $(t,u)\in R_{j}$. Let $k$ be the maximum of $i$ and $j$.
Then, by the preceding lemma, we have $(s,t),(t,u)\in R_{k}$.

By the definition of $R_{k+1}$ we have $(s,u)\in R_{k+1}$ so $(s,u)\in R^{+}$.
Hence $R^{+}$ is a transitive relation.

To show that $R^{+}$ is the smallest transitive relation containing $R$, i.e.
the *transitive closure* of $R$, let $B$ be a transitive relation containing
$R$. We will show that $R^{+}\subset B$.

To that end we will show that $R_{i}\subset B$ for each $i\in N$. Call a number
k splendid if $R_{k}\subset B$. By definition $R_{0}=R\subset B$ so zero is
spendid.

If $k$ is splendid we will show that $k+1$ is splendid as well. Assume $k$ is
splendid, i.e. $R_{k}\subset B$. Then for $(s,u)\in R_{k+1}\setminus R_{k}$ there is a
$t\in S$ such that $(s,t)\in R_{k}\subset B$ and $(t,u)\in R_{k}\subset B$.
Since $B$ is transitive we also $(s,u)\in B$. Hence $R_{k+1} \subset B$.

By the principle of mathematical induction all numbers are splendid.
